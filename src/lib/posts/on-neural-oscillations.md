During my PhD I spent a lot of time staring at spectrograms — time-frequency representations of brain signals recorded while people moved their hands, imagined movements, or simply waited. The dominant feature of those plots was almost always a band of power in the beta range (roughly 13–30 Hz) that would suppress during movement and rebound sharply afterwards.

This beta rebound, sometimes called event-related synchronisation, is one of the most reproducible findings in human motor neurophysiology. It shows up in MEG, EEG, and intracranial recordings. It correlates with performance. It changes with learning. And yet, after decades of research, there is still no consensus on what it actually *does*.

## Why oscillations are hard to interpret

The difficulty is partly methodological. Neural oscillations are measured at the population level — a sensor sitting outside (or even on the surface of) the skull integrates the activity of millions of neurons. The signal you capture is the sum of many processes, only some of which are rhythmic in any meaningful sense.

There is also a conceptual problem. It is tempting to treat a spectral peak as evidence of a *rhythm* — a clock-like process that coordinates neural activity. But a peak in the power spectrum is consistent with other generative mechanisms too, including noise filtered by the membrane time constants of neurons. The debate between "true oscillations" and "filtered noise" is older than it looks and is not fully resolved.

## What I found interesting

My own work focused on how beta oscillations in the motor system relate to the precision of movement. The short version: there is something there, but the relationship is not simple, and the effect sizes are modest enough that individual-level prediction is hard.

What stayed with me is less the specific findings and more the general lesson: complex systems produce signals that are simultaneously robust at the group level and highly variable at the individual level. That gap between population regularity and individual noise is where most of the interesting science lives — and it turns out to be just as relevant in the government data I work with now as it was in brain data.

That crossover is probably why I ended up here.
